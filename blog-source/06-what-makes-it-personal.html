There is a difference between a bandana with a name printed on it and a bandana that is genuinely theirs. The first one is a product. The second one is a small portrait of an animal you love.

Both are fine. But if you are commissioning something custom, it is worth knowing what actually makes the difference — and how to ask for it.

A name is a starting point, not the design

"Mochi" printed in the middle of a bandana is nice. "Mochi" printed in a soft rounded type, in the pale green that makes her cream coat glow, surrounded by the dandelions she has destroyed every summer of her life — that is a different object entirely.

The name is the anchor. What surrounds it is the actual gift.

The details that make a design land

When people send me a brief, the ones that produce the best work almost always contain some combination of these:

  • A physical detail. One white paw. A folded ear. A ridiculous tail. The thing you would describe first if a stranger asked you to point them out in a park.
  • A behaviour. Sleeps on her back with all four legs up. Screams at the postman. Will not walk past a puddle. This is what turns a drawing into a character.
  • An obsession. A specific toy, a specific ball, tennis balls in general, cheese, the hoover, one particular squirrel.
  • A colour you already know suits them. Most owners know this instinctively — you have seen them in a hundred photos.
  • An occasion, if there is one. A first birthday, a gotcha day, a new home, a wedding, a "she made it through the surgery".

The single most useful sentence anyone has ever sent me was: "He is a very serious dog who believes he is in charge of the whole street." I knew exactly what to draw before I had even seen a photo.

How to write a brief that gets you what you want

You do not need to be a designer, and you do not need to know what you want visually. You need to describe the animal. Three or four sentences is plenty.

A good brief usually covers:

  1. Who they are. Name, breed or best guess, rough age, coat colour.
  2. What they are like. The personality, in your own words. Be funny about it — the funny bits are the useful bits.
  3. Anything that must be in it. A favourite toy, a colour, a word, a date.
  4. Anything that must not be. Just as useful. "No hearts", "nothing pink", "please do not make him look cute, he would hate that."
  5. Where it is going. Everyday wear, a birthday, a gift for someone else.

And send photos. Two or three is ideal: one clear shot of the face in daylight, one of the whole animal standing, and one that captures the personality even if it is technically a terrible photo. That third one is often the most useful of the lot.

Things worth deciding before you order

Spelling. Check it twice. Nicknames are welcome — most pets have four names and answer to none of them — but tell me which one you want, exactly as you want it printed.

Length. Short names have real presence on fabric. Long names have to be set smaller, so if your dog is legally called Bartholomew but answers to Barty, Barty will look better.

One name or two. If two pets share a bandana, say which is which. If you would rather have one each, say that too — they usually photograph better as a pair.

Contrast. Pale text on a pale bandana looks beautiful in a design file and vanishes on a moving animal in a park. I will usually push you towards more contrast than you think you need.

What a gift needs that a personal order does not

If you are buying this for someone else, get me two extra things: the pet's neck measurement, and a photo the owner has not seen a hundred times. Nothing gives a surprise away faster than asking a friend to measure their own dog's neck for no reason.

And if you cannot get the measurement, say so — a tie-on in a middle size covers a lot of ground, and it is far better than guessing wrong.

Why it is worth the extra sentence

Every custom bandana I make takes the same amount of drawing time. The difference between a good one and one that makes someone cry a bit when they open it is almost never skill — it is information.

So use the box. Tell me the ridiculous thing your pet does. That is the bit that ends up in the drawing.
