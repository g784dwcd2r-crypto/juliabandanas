Printed cotton fails in a small number of predictable ways, and almost all of them happen in the first three washes. Get those right and a shirt keeps its print for years. Get them wrong and it goes chalky by March.

None of this is difficult. It is mostly about heat.

The short version

  • Cold wash, 30°C or below.
  • Inside out, always.
  • No fabric softener.
  • Hang to dry, out of direct sun.
  • Iron on the back, never on the print.

That is the whole thing. The rest of this explains why, which is useful when you are deciding whether a rule can be broken.

Why heat is the enemy

Most graphics on affordable printed cotton are either screen-printed plastisol or a heat-applied transfer. Both are, in effect, a thin layer of flexible plastic sitting on top of the fabric rather than dyed into it.

Plastic softens when it is warm. A 60°C wash makes the print pliable while it is being tumbled against a drum and against itself, and the surface picks up thousands of tiny cracks. Those cracks scatter light, which is what “faded” actually looks like on a print — it has not lost pigment, it has lost a smooth surface.

A tumble dryer is worse than a hot wash, because it applies heat and abrasion at the same time for forty minutes. If you change only one habit, change this one.

Why inside out matters more than it sounds

Turning a shirt inside out puts the print against the body of the garment rather than against the drum and the zip of whatever else is in the load. It removes most of the mechanical damage for zero effort.

It also helps the colour of the fabric itself. Dark cotton fades from the outside face; washing it inside out slows that considerably.

Why not fabric softener

Softener works by depositing a waxy film on fibres. On a printed area that film sits between the print and the fabric it is bonded to, and over repeated washes it contributes to lifting and peeling at the edges.

It also builds up in cotton generally, which is why old towels stop absorbing water. Half a cup of white vinegar in the rinse does the softening job without the film, if you want one.

Drying

Hang it. Inside out, on a line or an airer, away from direct sunlight — UV fades pigment in the fabric and makes plastisol brittle.

If you must use a dryer, use the lowest heat setting, take the garment out slightly damp and let it finish on a hanger. Most of the damage happens in the last ten minutes, when the fabric is dry and the drum keeps going.

Ironing

Turn it inside out and iron the back of the print. If you have to press the front, put a tea towel or a sheet of baking parchment over it and use a medium setting with no steam directly on the graphic.

An iron placed directly on plastisol will melt it onto the soleplate, ruining both. This happens to everyone once.

Socks and slippers

Socks are usually knitted with the pattern in the yarn rather than printed on top, so they are far more forgiving — a 30°C wash and a line dry keeps the colours and, importantly, the elastic. Heat is what kills sock cuffs.

Slippers with a printed or embroidered upper should be spot-cleaned rather than machine-washed. If the sole is glued rather than stitched, a full wash cycle will eventually separate them.

Mugs

Check the listing. Some printed mugs are dishwasher-safe and some are decidedly not, and the difference is whether the design is fired into the glaze or applied on top. When in doubt, wash by hand — a dishwasher's detergent is abrasive enough to strip a surface print in a few dozen cycles.

Stains

Cold water, immediately, from the back of the fabric so you push the stain out rather than through. A little washing-up liquid worked in gently with a finger handles most food and drink.

Never use hot water on an unknown stain and never use bleach anywhere near a print. Both are irreversible, and one of them is irreversible in an interesting new colour.
