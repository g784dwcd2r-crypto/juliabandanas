A print of a duck holding a glass of wine can go one of two ways in a room. It can look like a considered piece of art that happens to be funny, or it can look like something bought at an airport.

The difference is almost never the print. It is the framing, the height and what is around it.

Buy bigger than feels sensible

The single most common mistake with an affordable print is buying it too small. A 21×30cm print on a large wall reads as a postcard someone forgot to take down. The same image at 50×70cm reads as a decision.

A rough guide: a print hung above furniture should be somewhere between half and two-thirds the width of the thing below it. Above a standard sofa, that means 60cm wide at the absolute minimum, and 70cm is better. Above a bedside table, 30×40cm is fine.

Prices scale far more gently than sizes do, and the jump from small to properly sized is usually a few pounds. It is the cheapest improvement available.

Frame it like you mean it

Unframed prints look like posters. Framed prints look like art. This is unfair but it is true, and it is worth the extra money.

Three things that do most of the work:

  • A mount. A window mount — the card border between the print and the frame — is what separates a framed print from a poster in a frame. Even 4cm of white card transforms it.
  • A thin frame. Narrow black, natural oak or off-white. Wide ornate frames fight with an illustration; thin ones get out of the way.
  • Glass, not acrylic, if you can. Acrylic scratches and holds dust with static. Glass is heavier and worth it.

Standard sizes exist for a reason. Buying a print at 30×40cm or 50×70cm means high-street frames fit without custom work, which keeps the whole thing affordable.

Hang it lower than you want to

Almost everyone hangs pictures too high. The convention is that the centre of the image sits at eye level, roughly 145–150cm from the floor.

Above furniture the rule changes: leave 15–25cm between the top of the sofa or sideboard and the bottom of the frame. Any more and the picture floats, unconnected to anything.

Measure from the floor, not from the ceiling. Ceilings are not level and floors mostly are.

One print or several

A single well-sized print is almost always better than three small ones scattered about. If you do want a group, make it deliberate:

A pair, same size, same frame, hung with a consistent gap — 5 to 8cm — and their centres in a level line.

A grid of four or six identical sizes, treated as one large rectangle. Get the outer edges square and the eye forgives everything inside.

A gallery wall, which is much harder than it looks. Lay it out on the floor first, photograph it, adjust, and only then put a nail in a wall.

What does not work is three different sizes in three different frames, evenly spaced. That reads as indecision.

Where a funny print actually belongs

Kitchens, hallways, downstairs loos, home offices and the wall beside a dining table. Rooms people move through, or sit in and talk.

Where it does not belong: directly above a bed, which is where you want something quiet, and as the only thing on a large blank wall in a formal sitting room, where it will look stranded.

The best test is whether it makes you glance at it and think yes, good a month after you hung it. Almost nothing else matters.
