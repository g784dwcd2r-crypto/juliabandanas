Graphic t-shirts are ordered in the wrong size more than any other garment, and it is not because people do not know their size. It is because "medium" means five different things depending on who made the shirt.

The fix is to stop reading the letter and start reading the measurements.

Measure a t-shirt you already own

Not your chest. A t-shirt.

Find the shirt in your wardrobe that fits exactly how you like, lay it flat on a table, smooth it out, and measure two things:

  • Chest width: straight across from armpit seam to armpit seam.
  • Body length: from the highest point of the shoulder straight down to the hem.

Those two numbers are your size, in every shop, forever. Compare them against the size chart on the product page and buy whichever letter matches. It takes ninety seconds and it removes the guesswork entirely.

Note that chest width is measured flat and single-layer, so a 52cm chest width is a 104cm garment circumference. Size charts sometimes quote one and sometimes the other. If the numbers look absurdly small, they are flat measurements.

Rough guide, if you have nothing to measure

SizeChest width (flat)Typical UK fit
S46–48cmUK 8–10 / chest 88–94cm
M50–52cmUK 10–12 / chest 96–102cm
L54–56cmUK 14–16 / chest 104–110cm
XL58–60cmUK 18–20 / chest 112–118cm

Treat this as a starting point, not gospel. The measurements on the individual product page always win.

The Asian-sizing problem, stated plainly

A lot of printed graphic tees are made to Asian sizing, which runs roughly one to two sizes smaller than UK sizing for the same letter. An Asian XL is often a UK L, sometimes a UK M.

This is not a trick and it is not a quality issue — it is just a different chart. But it is why the measurements matter more than the letter, and it is why "I'm always a medium" is the sentence that precedes most returns.

If a product lists centimetres, use them. If it does not, assume it runs small and size up.

Cotton weight, and what it tells you

T-shirt fabric is quoted in gsm — grams per square metre. It is the single best indicator of how a shirt will feel and last.

  • 120–140gsm: light and drapey. Cool in summer, semi-transparent in pale colours, wears out faster.
  • 150–180gsm: the standard. Good balance of weight, opacity and durability. Most printed tees sit here.
  • 190–220gsm: heavyweight. Holds its shape, does not cling, feels substantially more expensive. Warmer to wear.

If you have ever bought a graphic tee that felt disappointing without being able to say why, it was almost certainly under 140gsm.

Boxy or fitted

Two shapes dominate, and the print sits differently on each.

Boxy / oversized: wider body, dropped shoulder seam sitting partway down the arm, shorter relative length. Large prints look right on these. This is the current default for graphic tees and it is why they can feel unexpectedly wide.

Classic / fitted: shoulder seam at the actual shoulder, tapered body. Better for smaller prints and for anyone who does not want a lot of fabric.

You cannot tell which you are getting from the size letter. You can tell from the body length: a boxy tee is short relative to its width, a classic tee is long relative to its width.

Shrinkage

Cotton shrinks in length more than width, and most of it happens in the first wash. A 100% cotton tee washed at 30°C and air dried loses very little — maybe 1–2cm in length. The same shirt tumble-dried hot can lose 4–5cm, which turns a good fit into a crop top.

If a shirt is borderline, wash cool and air dry and it will stay where it is.

Keeping the print alive

Inside out, 30°C, no fabric softener, no tumble dryer, and never iron directly on the print. Iron on the reverse if you must, or put a tea towel over it.

Those five rules are most of what determines whether a graphic tee still looks good in three years. The reasoning is in washing printed cotton.

Common questions

How do I know what size graphic t-shirt to buy?

Measure a t-shirt you already like flat across the chest, armpit seam to armpit seam, and match that number to the size chart. Ignore the letter.

Do printed t-shirts shrink?

Cotton shrinks mostly in length, mostly in the first wash, and mostly because of heat. Wash at 30°C and air dry and shrinkage is negligible.

What gsm is a good t-shirt?

150–180gsm for everyday wear. Below 140gsm feels thin and can be see-through in pale colours; above 190gsm feels premium but is warmer.

Should I size up in a graphic tee?

If the shirt uses Asian sizing, or if you want the oversized look, yes. If the product page gives centimetres, use those instead of guessing.
