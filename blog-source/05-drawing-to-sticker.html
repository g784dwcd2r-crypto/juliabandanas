People occasionally ask how long a sticker takes to make, expecting an answer like "twenty minutes". The drawing takes twenty minutes. Everything after the drawing takes a fortnight.

Here is the whole route from a doodle to a thing in a padded envelope.

1. It starts badly, on purpose

Almost everything begins as a scribble in a sketch file I never show anyone. A duck wearing a bucket hat. A snail with a very slight attitude problem. Most of them are terrible and stay terrible.

The test is simple and completely unscientific: I leave it for a week, open it again, and see whether it still makes me laugh. If it does not, it does not survive. That is roughly one in five.

2. Redrawing it properly

The survivors get drawn again from scratch, at proper size, with the rules the whole shop follows:

  • One consistent line weight, so a duck and a mushroom look like they come from the same place.
  • Flat colour only — no gradients, no soft shading. It prints cleaner and it reads better at 4cm across.
  • The same pastel palette every time: soft pink, soft yellow, soft green, and black for everything else.
  • A face, wherever a face can reasonably go.

The palette is the reason ten unrelated categories still look like one shop. Constraints do far more for a small brand than variety does.

3. Working out what it wants to be

Not every drawing suits every product, and this is the part that took me longest to learn.

Stickers need a clear outline and no fine detail — anything thinner than about half a millimetre disappears when it is cut. Prints can carry far more detail, and often get extra background that the sticker version cannot afford. Keyrings and pins need a shape that reads instantly at thumbnail size, which is why they are almost always a single character, no scene. Bandanas and totes want a repeating pattern rather than one big drawing, so the design gets rebuilt as a tile.

So one duck can become four quite different files.

4. Proofing, which is where the surprises live

Screens lie. A pastel yellow that glows on an iPad can come back looking like weak tea on uncoated card, and pink is worse — it shifts warm on almost every stock I have tried.

So everything gets a test print before it goes anywhere near the shop. I check three things: whether the colours survived, whether the black outline is still crisp, and whether the face still reads from across a room. A surprising number of designs fail that third one and get their eyes moved a millimetre apart.

5. Cutting and finishing

Sticker sheets are cut with a slight offset around each drawing — the little white border is deliberate, not laziness. It protects the printed edge and it makes each one far easier to peel with a fingernail.

Then everything is checked by hand. Anything with a misregistered cut, a scuff or a colour that drifted goes in the seconds box, which is how the occasional discounted mystery bag comes about.

6. Into an envelope

Stickers go in a glassine sleeve with a backing board so they arrive flat. Prints get a cellophane sleeve and a rigid mailer. Everything ships in a recycled paper mailer with paper tape — no plastic, which took three suppliers to get right and is worth the faff.

So: a fortnight

Roughly two weeks from a scribble to something you can hold, most of it spent waiting on proofs and squinting at printed sheets in daylight.

It is a ludicrous amount of process for a small drawing of a duck. I have made peace with that.
