You have a beautiful bandana on a beautiful animal and thirty seconds before they lie down and refuse to participate. Here is how to get the shot in that window.

Get the light right and everything else gets easier

Light does more for a photo than any other single thing, and the good news is you do not need equipment — you need a time of day and a direction.

  • Shoot outside, in shade. Open shade — the shadow side of a building, under a tree, a north-facing garden — is the most flattering light there is. Soft, even, no squinting.
  • Or shoot in the hour after sunrise or before sunset. Low golden light makes pastel colours glow and puts a warm rim around the fur.
  • Avoid midday sun. It carves hard shadows under the chin and blows out pale bandanas into featureless white.
  • Indoors, use one big window and turn every lamp off. Put your pet side-on to the window, not facing it. Mixing daylight and yellow bulbs makes colours go strange.

Never use flash on an animal. It flattens the fur, gives them alien eyes, and teaches them that cameras are unpleasant.

Get down to their level

The single most common mistake is standing up and pointing the phone down. That angle makes any animal look small and slightly guilty, and it hides the bandana completely — from above, all you see is the top edge.

Kneel. Sit. Lie on the floor if the situation calls for it. Photograph from their eye height or just below, and the bandana sits in the frame exactly the way it sits in real life.

Sort the background before you sort the pet

Pick your spot first, then bring the animal to it. Look for a plain wall, a hedge, a wooden fence, long grass — anything without a bin, a radiator or a dangling lead in it.

Then put a few metres between your pet and that background. Distance is what gives you the soft blur, even on a phone. A dog sitting against a wall gets a sharp, busy backdrop; the same dog three metres in front of the same wall gets a lovely creamy one.

On colour: a pastel pink bandana against pink blossom disappears. Against green grass or a dark fence it sings. Choose contrast.

Phone settings worth two minutes of your life

  • Tap to focus on the eyes. Every time. A photo with sharp eyes and a slightly soft nose looks professional; the reverse looks broken.
  • Then drag the exposure slider down slightly. Phones overexpose pale fur and pale fabric. Dark coats often need it nudged up instead.
  • Use portrait mode carefully. It is excellent on a still, side-on animal and terrible with ears and whiskers, which it tends to slice off. If in doubt, shoot normally.
  • Use burst mode. Hold the shutter and take thirty frames. One of them will have both ears up and the tongue in.
  • Use the main lens, not the ultra-wide. Wide lenses distort noses into snouts.

Getting them to look

Get everything ready first — light, background, focus — and only then get their attention. You have about three seconds of genuine interest, so do not waste it while you are still finding the right angle.

What works, roughly in order: a squeaky toy held just above the lens; a new sound they have never heard, made once; their name in a voice you never normally use; a treat held at the lens, not above your head. What does not work: repeating their name eight times. By the fourth, it means nothing.

Then have a second person. Genuinely — one person holding the toy and one holding the camera is worth more than any lens.

Make sure the bandana looks like the bandana

Straighten it just before you shoot. Check the point is centred and the tie is at the back. Turn your pet slightly side-on rather than square to the camera — three-quarters is the angle where fabric drapes and reads properly.

And take one deliberate close-up of the design itself. When the name is printed on there, you want a frame where it is actually legible.

Editing: less than you think

Lift the shadows a little, pull the highlights down a little, add a touch of contrast, and stop. Heavy filters wreck pastels — pink goes orange and green goes grey. If you crop, crop to square or 4:5, because that is what social media will do anyway and you may as well choose which bits survive.

And the boring but important part

Never leave a bandana on for a photo session and then forget about it. Take it off afterwards, especially before car journeys, crates and unsupervised play.
