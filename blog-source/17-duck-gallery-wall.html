One print on a wall is a decision. Six prints on a wall is a project, and it is where most people's confidence runs out.

This is the method that works, and it is mostly arithmetic rather than taste.

Start on the floor, not the wall

Lay everything out on the floor in front of the wall it is going on. Move things around until it looks right, photograph it, and only then pick up a hammer.

Ten minutes on the floor saves an hour of holes. This is the entire secret and everyone skips it.

Pick one of three shapes

The grid. Identical frames, identical sizes, even gaps. Formal, calm, and by far the easiest to hang because everything is measured. Best in hallways, above a desk, and anywhere you want it to look deliberate rather than collected.

The salon hang. Mixed sizes filling an irregular block. Looks casual, is the hardest to do well. The trick is that the outer edges of the whole arrangement form a clean rectangle even though the interior is irregular.

The line. A single row, centres all at the same height, above a sofa or along a corridor. Underrated. It takes three prints and one afternoon and it always looks right.

If you have never done this before, do the line. If you have six identical small prints, do the grid.

The numbers

MeasurementUse
145–150cmHeight to the centre of the arrangement. Gallery standard, and it works
5–8cmGap between frames. Consistent throughout, whatever the sizes
15–25cmGap between the bottom of the frames and the top of a sofa or sideboard
~60%How much of the furniture's width the arrangement should span. Wider looks better than narrower

The 145–150cm figure is measured to the centre of the whole group, not the centre of any individual frame. That is the bit people get wrong, and it is why arrangements end up hung too high.

Almost every gallery wall in a home is hung too high. If you are unsure, go lower. Art is meant to be at eye level for someone standing, not for someone on a stepladder.

Framing small prints so they look like art

A mount — the card border between the print and the frame — is what separates a framed print from a poster in a frame. It costs a few pounds and does more for the look than a more expensive frame would.

  • Go wider than feels necessary. A 5cm mount on an A5 print looks generous and intentional. A 2cm mount looks like an accident.
  • Off-white, not brilliant white, unless the print's own background is pure white.
  • Weight the bottom slightly. Traditional framing makes the bottom border a little deeper than the top, because the eye reads an even border as bottom-heavy. A few millimetres is enough.

For frames: pick one finish and stay with it across the whole wall. Mixed frame finishes are an advanced move and mostly go wrong. Black, natural oak, or thin white — choose one.

Mixing subjects without it becoming a gift shop

Six duck prints in a row is a lot of ducks. The arrangements that work best use ducks as roughly half the wall and let the rest be quieter — a plain typographic print, a photograph, something abstract in a matching palette.

The unifying element should be colour, not subject. If everything shares a warm yellow and a soft green, the wall reads as one thing even when the images have nothing to do with each other.

There is more on this — the general principle of buying illustrated prints without your home looking like a shop — in hanging a print.

Hanging without wrecking the plaster

Use paper templates. Cut a rectangle of newspaper for each frame, mark where the hook falls on each one, and tape them to the wall in your arrangement. Stand back, adjust, then drill through the paper.

For light frames on solid walls, adhesive strips are genuinely fine and remove cleanly if you pull the tab straight down and slowly. For anything with glass over A4, use a proper fixing — a failed adhesive strip at two in the morning is a memorable way to be woken up.

Common questions

How high should a gallery wall be hung?

The centre of the whole arrangement at 145–150cm from the floor. Not the centre of one frame — the centre of the group.

How much space should be between frames?

5–8cm, and the same gap everywhere regardless of frame size. Consistent spacing is what makes mixed sizes look deliberate.

Should all the frames match?

For a first attempt, yes — one finish throughout. Mixed frames can work when the prints are very varied, but it is harder than it looks and matching is never wrong.

How many prints do I need?

Three for a line, six or nine for a grid, seven or more for a salon hang. Odd numbers in a row, even numbers in a grid.
