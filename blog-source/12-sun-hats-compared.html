Three hats, three different jobs. People tend to own one and use it for everything, which is why the answer to "which hat should I take" is usually "not that one".

Here is the honest comparison, based on what each one is actually good at.

If you want one hat for everything, it is the bucket hat. If you want the right hat for a specific day, read on.

Bucket hats: the default

The all-round winner, and the reason most of a season goes into them. A bucket hat gives you 360 degrees of brim, which means your ears and the back of your neck are covered — the two places people reliably burn and never think about.

They pack flat, they survive being crushed in a rucksack, and they hold their position by contact with the whole head rather than by a strap, so they do not slide.

Where they lose: the brim is short. In genuinely strong midday sun, a 6cm brim does less for your face than a 10cm one. And they are sized rather than adjustable, so the measurement matters — the fitting guide is two minutes well spent.

Best for: travel, festivals, walking, anything where the hat spends half the day in a bag. Bucket hats and fisherman hats.

Baseball caps: the practical one

A cap is the hat you can wear while doing something. The peak keeps sun and rain directly out of your eyes better than any round brim, it does not catch on a car doorframe, and the adjustable strap at the back means fit is essentially solved.

If you are between hat sizes, or buying for someone whose head you have never measured, a cap is the safe purchase.

Where they lose: ears and neck, completely unprotected. A day of gardening in a cap is a day of burnt ears. If you are outside for hours, a cap alone is not sun protection.

Best for: driving, sport, gifts where you do not know a size, anywhere you need clear peripheral vision. Caps.

Straw hats: the specialist

A wide brim beats everything else for shade, and a woven straw crown lets heat out in a way that cotton does not. On a genuinely hot, still day, a straw hat is measurably more comfortable than either of the others.

Where they lose: everywhere else. They do not pack — a crushed straw hat stays crushed. They catch wind like a sail. And they are not waterproof; wet straw softens, loses shape, and can go permanently misshapen if it dries badly.

Best for: beaches, gardens, weddings, holidays where the hat travels on your head rather than in a case.

What actually matters for sun protection

Three things, roughly in this order.

  1. Brim width. The single biggest factor. Around 7cm all the way round is the usual recommendation for real face, ear and neck coverage. Most bucket hats sit a little under that; most straw hats sit comfortably over it.
  2. Weave density. Hold the hat up to a light. If you can see through it, UV goes through it too. Tightly woven cotton beats loosely woven straw, which is the one place a bucket hat can outperform a wide brim.
  3. Colour. Darker fabric absorbs more UV before it reaches you, but also gets hotter. Pale crowns are cooler; dark crowns block marginally more. In practice, weave matters more than colour.

None of them protect your nose from light bouncing off sand, water or pavement, which is why sunscreen is still involved regardless of what is on your head.

Picking one for the actual day

  • City break, lots of walking, bag with you: bucket hat.
  • Beach, sitting still, hot: straw hat.
  • Driving somewhere, in and out of the car: cap.
  • Windy coastal walk: cap, or a bucket hat that fits properly.
  • Unpredictable British summer: bucket hat, because it is the only one that does not mind rain.
  • Gift for someone whose size you do not know: cap.

Common questions

Which hat is best for sun protection?

A wide-brimmed hat with a tight weave — usually a straw hat — covers the most skin. A bucket hat is a close second and far more practical, because it protects ears and neck and survives being packed. A baseball cap protects only your face.

Is a bucket hat better than a cap?

For coverage and packing, yes. For fit, wind and clear vision, the cap wins. Most people are better served by owning both and choosing by the day.

Can you wear a straw hat in the rain?

Avoid it. Straw absorbs water, softens, and can dry out of shape. If it gets caught in a shower, reshape it and dry it slowly at room temperature, away from a radiator.

What brim width do you need for real shade?

About 7cm all the way round is the usual figure for covering the face, ears and neck. Anything under 5cm is a style choice rather than a sun measure.
