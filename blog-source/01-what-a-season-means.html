Julia Mind does not have a permanent range. It has a season, and the season is the whole shop.

Every three months one subject is chosen and everything is rebuilt around it. Not a themed shelf tucked into the corner of a general store — the entire catalogue. When the quarter ends, the range is replaced rather than extended, and what sold out stays sold out.

Season 01 is ducks. Here is how the arrangement actually works, and what it means if you are buying something.

Why a season, and not a category

Most small shops grow by addition. You start with one good idea, it works, so you add an adjacent one. Two years later you are running a warehouse of things you no longer have an opinion about, and the original good idea is buried on page four.

Running in seasons forces the opposite. Because everything gets replaced, nothing gets to coast. A piece has to earn its place against a single question — does this belong in a shop that is currently about ducks? — and if the answer is “not really, but it might sell”, it does not go in.

The constraint is the point. Working within one subject for three months produces a set that hangs together rather than a list that accumulates.

What a season contains

Roughly the same shape each time, worked through whatever the subject happens to be:

  • Things you wear on your head. Bucket hats, caps, the occasional oddity. Historically the largest part of a season and the fastest to sell.
  • Things you wear on your body. Shirts, tees, socks, slippers.
  • Things you put on a wall. Prints, in sizes from postcard-ish up to something that genuinely fills a space.
  • Things for a desk, a kitchen or a car. Mugs, cases, small ornaments, whatever the subject suggests.

Not every season fills every slot. Some subjects simply do not want to be socks, and forcing them produces the kind of product nobody is pleased to receive.

What happens when a season closes

Three things, in this order.

First, stock stops being replenished. Everything in a season is bought once. When a size or a colourway goes, it is gone — there is no second order, because the second order would arrive after the season has ended.

Second, the remaining pieces stay on sale until they run out or the next season opens, whichever comes first.

Third, the site changes. New subject, new colour, new photography, new writing. The old season is not archived into a clearance bin; it simply stops.

This is worth knowing before you leave something in a basket for a fortnight. It is not artificial scarcity — it is just what happens when you buy once and do not reorder.

How a subject gets picked

Partly instinct, partly evidence. A subject has to survive three tests:

Does it read at 3cm? If it does not work embroidered on a cap or printed small on a sock, half the season is already impossible.

Does it survive being framed? Prints are where a subject either grows up or falls apart. Plenty of things are funny on a t-shirt and embarrassing on a wall.

Is there more than one joke in it? This is the one that kills most candidates. A subject needs enough range that forty pieces do not all say the same thing.

Ducks pass all three comfortably, which is why they went first. A duck reads instantly at any size, it is genuinely pleasant framed above a table, and there are enough variations — the smug ones, the cowboy ones, the ones in bucket hats, the rubber ones on a dashboard — that a whole quarter does not run out of ideas.

Suggesting one

Subjects get chosen partly from what people ask for, and this is not a polite fiction. If a suggestion keeps arriving, it moves up.

The season letter goes out twice a quarter — once when a season opens, once when it is about to close — and replying to it is the most direct route. Otherwise, the contact form reaches the same inbox.
