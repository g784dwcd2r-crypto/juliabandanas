Everything in a season is chosen, photographed and checked before it goes on sale. That sentence appears on a lot of shop pages and means very little, so here is what it actually involves.

The first pass: does it belong in this season

Most things are rejected here, and it takes about ten seconds each.

The question is not “is this good” but “is this good and about the subject”. A perfectly nice bucket hat with a small embroidered duck on the side is a maybe. A perfectly nice bucket hat with a generic slogan is a no, however well made, because it does not belong to the season and its presence would make the whole set feel like a bin.

This is why seasons are relatively small. The subject filter removes about nine tenths of what is available.

The second pass: the photographs

Supplier photography is where most problems hide. A few things get looked at hard:

  • Is the same product in every image? Listings frequently mix colourways, and occasionally mix products entirely.
  • Is there a scale reference? An ornament photographed alone on white can be 3cm or 30cm. If the listing does not say and the images do not show, it gets measured or dropped.
  • Are the colours plausible? Heavily saturated product shots produce a yellow that arrives as mustard.
  • Is the print placement consistent? On shirts especially, the position of a graphic can vary between the photograph and the garment.

Where the images do not answer a question, the listing text has to, and where neither does, the product does not go in.

The third pass: the description

Supplier titles are written for search engines, not people. They arrive looking like this:

Unisex Men's Cosplay Shirts 3D Prints Splicing Cartoon Cute Duck Printing Short Sleeve Shirt Fashion Hawaiian Shirts For Men

That is not a name, it is a keyword list. Where a product is important enough to the season it gets a real name; where it is not, the title is at least trimmed to something readable, and the type of thing it is — Hat, Shirt, Print — is set properly so the shop can group it sensibly.

The same goes for sizing. A listing that says “one size” without a measurement is nearly useless, so the measurement gets found and put where you can see it.

The fourth pass: the variants

This is the least glamorous part and it matters more than any of it.

Some products arrive with eighty variants — every colour crossed with every size — and a good proportion of those combinations do not really exist or have a stock count of one. Unpicking that is the difference between an order that ships and an order that gets cancelled a week later with an apology.

Where a product carries dozens of options, the shop shows them as a dropdown rather than a wall of buttons, because forty swatches side by side is not a choice, it is a puzzle.

What is not claimed

A few things this shop does not pretend about, because the claims are so common that not making them is worth stating.

These are not hand-made. They are chosen, checked and sold, and the making happened somewhere else — which is true of nearly every shop of this size, whatever the About page says.

Stock levels are real but they move. Where a listing shows a low number it usually is low, and where it shows thousands it genuinely is a warehouse rather than a shelf.

Delivery estimates are estimates. Two working days to dispatch is the commitment; what happens after that belongs to the postal service.

When something is wrong anyway

Occasionally it will be. The size chart will be off, or the colour will not match, or something will arrive damaged.

The policy is simple and does not involve arguing: thirty days, unworn, original packaging, message first. Photographs help but are not demanded. Nobody is going to be asked to prove they are disappointed.
