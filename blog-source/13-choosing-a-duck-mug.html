A mug is the most-used object most people own, and the one they think about least when buying. It is worth ten minutes, because the difference between a mug you like and a mug you tolerate is roughly one thousand cups of tea.

Capacity, and what the numbers mean

Mug capacity is quoted in millilitres and almost always refers to the brim-full volume, not the amount of tea that will actually be in it. Fill to a sensible level and you lose 40–60ml.

The honest advice: 330ml is right for most people, and 400ml+ is right for anyone who habitually reheats their tea in the microwave. If that is you, buy the bigger one and drink faster.

The handle is the thing people get wrong

Pick up a mug you already like and look at what your hand does. Most people hook one or two fingers through and rest the thumb on top. That needs a handle with enough gap to take two fingers — about 30mm of clear space.

Narrow, decorative handles look better in photographs and are worse to hold, particularly when the mug is full and hot. If a mug is going to be used daily, the handle matters more than the print.

Check also that the handle is set low enough. A handle mounted high on the body makes a full mug want to tip forward, which you feel every single time.

Ceramic, and why some mugs stay hot

Most printed mugs are stoneware or porcelain. The practical difference is wall thickness.

Thick walls hold heat longer and feel substantial. They also take heat out of the drink at the start, so the first sip is cooler.

Thin walls let you taste the drink sooner and lose heat faster. Better for coffee you drink quickly, worse for tea you nurse through a meeting.

Neither is better. Thick is the right answer for a desk mug; thin is the right answer for a morning coffee.

How the printing works, and what that means for you

Printed mugs come in two kinds, and they behave differently in a dishwasher.

Sublimation printing bonds the ink into a polymer coating on the mug. Vivid, photographic, capable of covering the whole body. Durable, but the coating is the weak point — abrasive scourers and very hot, high-alkaline dishwasher cycles will dull it over years.

Decal or transfer printing applies a ceramic image and fires it on. Slightly less photographic, considerably tougher. This is what "dishwasher safe" usually means on a mug that says it confidently.

Either way, the survival rules are the same, and they are in how to keep a printed mug looking new.

Colour, and where the duck sits

Two broad approaches in the mugs and cups range, and they suit different people.

A coloured body with a small duck. Deep navy, pondweed green, sky blue. Reads as a nice mug first and a duck mug second. This is the one to buy for someone whose kitchen you have seen and whose taste you are trying to respect.

A white body with a large print. The duck is the point. Better as a gift for a committed duck person, better in photographs, and slightly more likely to end up as the one specific mug somebody insists on.

If you are buying for a shared kitchen at work, go with the coloured body. A mug that is obviously somebody's is less likely to be taken; a mug that is obviously a joke is more likely to be chipped.

Buying more than one

If you are building a set, buy the same shape in different colours rather than different shapes in the same colour. Mixed colours in a matching shape look deliberate on a shelf. Mixed shapes look like a car boot sale, however nice each individual mug is.

Common questions

What size mug is standard?

Around 300–350ml. That gives roughly 290ml of actual drink, which is one normal cup of tea or coffee.

Are printed mugs dishwasher safe?

Usually yes, and they still last longer hand-washed. The damage comes from very hot cycles, harsh detergent tablets and mugs knocking together on the rack, not from water.

Can you microwave a printed mug?

Yes, unless it has any metallic detail — a gold rim, a metallic ink. Those spark, and that is not a small problem. Plain ceramic with a printed image is fine.

Why does my tea go cold so quickly?

Thin walls and a wide opening. A tall, thick-walled mug with a narrower mouth holds heat noticeably longer than a wide, shallow one of the same volume.

What is the best mug to give as a gift?

A 330–400ml mug with a two-finger handle in a colour rather than white. It is useful, it is unsized, and it does not assume anything about the recipient's kitchen.
