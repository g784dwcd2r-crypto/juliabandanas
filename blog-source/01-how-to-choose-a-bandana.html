A bandana is a small thing, which is exactly why the details matter. Get it right and it sits like it has always belonged there. Get it wrong and it either rides up into a scarf, slips round to the wrong side, or spends the afternoon in a hedge.

Here is everything I have learned making them, in the order you actually need it.

1. Start with the neck, not the size chart

Almost everyone starts by guessing from their pet's weight. Weight tells you very little — two 12kg dogs can have completely different necks. Take a soft tape measure, wrap it where a collar would sit, and write that number down. Add about 5cm for the tie. That is the number that decides everything else.

No tape measure? Use a shoelace or a phone charging cable, mark it with your thumb, then hold it against a ruler.

If you are between two sizes, always go up. A tie-on bandana can be tied tighter. It cannot be made bigger.

2. Match the shape to the animal

Bandanas come in more shapes than people realise, and the shape does most of the work:

  • Long-necked dogs — greyhounds, whippets, collies — suit a deeper triangle. There is room for it to hang properly, and a shallow one looks lost.
  • Flat-faced breeds — pugs, frenchies, bulldogs — do much better with a shallow cut that sits high. A deep one bunches under the chin and gets chewed.
  • Very small dogs and puppies want something light and short, or the fabric outweighs the dog.
  • Cats want a slip-on collar style, always, on a breakaway collar. Never a tie-on. This one is not negotiable.

3. Pick the fastening honestly

There are three, and each suits a different household.

Tie-on is the classic. It is adjustable, it costs less, and it looks the most relaxed. It also needs re-tying, and a determined dog can work it loose.

Slip-on threads onto the collar they already wear. It never comes off in the park, and it is the safest option for anything that goes off-lead. The trade-off is that you have to take the collar off to change it.

Snap-on has a popper or buckle. Quick to change, good for pets who hate faff, but the fastening is one more thing that can fail.

If your dog swims, digs, or wrestles other dogs, get a slip-on. If your dog mostly does pavements and cafés, tie-on is lovely.

4. Fabric matters more than you would think

Cotton is the safest bet. It breathes, it washes at low temperatures, and printed colour holds its depth on it far longer than it does on polyester. Cotton also softens as it ages rather than going shiny.

Look for something around 140–200gsm. Lighter than that and it feels like a napkin. Heavier and it stands away from the neck instead of draping.

Avoid anything with a stiff coating, loose fringing, or beads. Fringing gets chewed and beads get swallowed.

5. Choose colour against their coat, not with it

This is the mistake I see most often. People pick a colour that matches the dog, and the dog disappears into it.

  • Black and dark coats — pastel yellow and soft pink are extraordinary on them. Cream and white also glow.
  • Golden, cream and ginger coats — pastel green and soft blue give the contrast that yellow cannot.
  • White and grey coats — anything works, so go with the pet's personality instead. Pink for the dramatic ones, green for the outdoorsy ones.
  • Merle, brindle and tabby — go plain or near-plain. A busy pattern on a busy coat becomes noise.

6. Then think about where it will actually be worn

A bandana for daily walks, a bandana for a wedding, and a bandana for a birthday photo are three different objects. Daily wear wants a colour that hides mud. A photo bandana can be as pale and impractical as you like, because it only has to survive twenty minutes.

Most people are better off with two: one good one, and one they do not mind ruining.

7. And finally — the safety bit

Two fingers should slide comfortably under the tie. If they do not, it is too tight. Take it off for crates, car journeys, unsupervised play and sleep. It is an accessory, not a collar, and it is not meant to be worn twenty-four hours a day.

That is genuinely all of it. Measure the neck, match the shape, be honest about the fastening, pick cotton, and choose a colour that fights their coat rather than agreeing with it.
